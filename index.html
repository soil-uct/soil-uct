Unconfined Compression Test of Soil

Agricultural Soil Mechanics for undergraduate Biosystems Mechanical Engineering students

From lab to field: we measure soil strength and use it in machinery decisions.

Why do tractors on wet fields leave deep ruts and lower yields?

Wet soil is weak, and one simple test tells us how weak.
  • Wheel stress greater than soil strength → compaction (Håkansson & Reeder, 1994)
  • Compaction → short, thick roots and less water uptake
  • The unconfined compression test gives the strength number

Soil: three phases

Figure 1: Soil: three phases

Soil = solids + water + air
  • More water → lower strength
  • Solids carry the load; water and air fill the voids between particles
  • Water content $w$ = mass of water ÷ mass of dry solids
  • Bulk density $\rho$ = total mass ÷ total volume; dry density $\rho_d=\rho/(1+w)$
  • Looser packing means lower dry density and weaker soil
  • Saturated soil: water fills all the voids (no air)

The Unconfined Compression Test (UCT)

Figure 2: The unconfined test

Load from the top, no lateral pressure ($\sigma_3=0$)
  • Cylindrical specimen, length 2 to 2.5 times the diameter (L/D)
  • The peak axial stress is the unconfined compressive strength $q_u$
  • No peak? Take the stress at 15% strain as $q_u$ (ASTM D2166)
  • Quick and cheap, but only for cohesive soil

Stress and strain

Figure 3: Stress and strain

Stress: force per unit area
  • Same force, smaller area → higher stress
  • Strain: percentage shortening
  • Strain is often given in %; stress in kPa (1 kPa = 1 kN/m²)

Test apparatus

Figure 4: Test apparatus

Force + displacement = data
  • Load cell → P
  • LVDT (Linear Variable Differential Transformer) → ΔL

Test procedure

Figure 6: Test procedure

Six steps from sample to data
  • Strain rate: ≈ 1% per minute (≈ 0.76 mm/min for L = 76 mm)
  • Stop: load drops or ε = 15% (about 15 minutes)
  • Run at least three replicates
  • Next slides: each step in detail

Step 1 · Prepare the specimen

Figure 7: Trimming the ends

Undisturbed sample

  • Push a thin-walled tube into the soil, or cut a block
  • Extrude slowly, in the direction it entered
  • Trim with a wire saw or soil lathe
  • Wrap in foil or wax if it must wait

Remolded sample

  • Adjust the soil to the target water content
  • Compact in layers in a mold to the target density
  • Extrude and trim in the same way
  • Use it to compare moisture or density effects

Size rule: diameter of at least 33 mm (our example: 38 mm); L/D between 2 and 2.5 (example: L = 76 mm); largest particle no more than one tenth of the diameter. Both ends flat and square to the axis.

Step 2 · Measure the specimen

Figure 8: Calipers at three positions

  • Diameter: top, middle and bottom, then average to get $D_0$
  • Length: three places around the specimen, then average to get $L_0$
  • Mass: weigh immediately, to 0.1 g
  • Check L/D; keep the trimmings for a water-content check
$$A_0=\frac{\pi D_0^2}{4}=\frac{\pi(38)^2}{4}\approx 1134\ \text{mm}^2$$
$$\rho=\frac{m}{A_0L_0}$$

A large difference between the three diameters means the specimen is not a clean cylinder. Trim again or discard it.

Step 3 · Set up in the frame

Figure 9: Seating the top platen

  1. Put the specimen on the lower platen, centered on the marked circles.
  2. Raise the platen until the top plate almost touches. Check that the axis is vertical and the plates are parallel.
  3. Make a light contact only, with a seating load of a few newtons at most.
  4. Zero the load cell and the LVDT at this point.
  5. Start the datalogger and confirm that both channels respond.

Do this quickly. A specimen left in open air loses water and appears stronger than it is.

Step 4 · Loading

Figure 10: Loading and the live curve

$$\text{rate}=\dot\varepsilon\,L_0=0.01\times 76=0.76\ \text{mm/min}$$
  • Allowed range: 0.5 to 2% of $L_0$ per minute
  • Peak at about 4% strain: roughly 4 minutes
  • 15% strain: roughly 15 minutes
  • Start the drive and keep the speed constant
  • Log every 10 to 15 s, giving about 16 to 24 points before the peak at 4 min
  • Watch the specimen: cracks, bulging, a shear plane

Do not change the speed during the test. Faster loading gives a different, usually higher, strength.

Step 5 · Log data and stop

Figure 11: Recording the failure

Peak found

Continue until the load clearly falls with increasing strain. The highest stress is $q_u$.

No peak

Stop at ε = 15%. Take $q_u$ as the stress at 15% strain.

After stopping

Unload, photograph the specimen, sketch the failure plane and measure its angle if visible.

The failure pattern (shear plane, barreling, vertical cracks) tells you how far to trust the number.

Step 6 · Water content

Figure 12: Drying to constant mass

  • Place the whole specimen (or a representative part, including the failure zone) in a pre-weighed can
  • Weigh at once: wet mass
  • Dry at 110 ± 5 °C (ASTM D2216; 105 °C is also widely used) to constant mass, usually 12 to 24 h
  • Cool, then weigh: dry mass
$$w=\frac{m_{wet}-m_{dry}}{m_{dry}}\times 100\%$$
$$\rho_d=\frac{\rho}{1+w}\;\;(w\ \text{as a decimal})$$

Report $q_u$ together with w and the dry density. Without them the strength number cannot be compared.

Failure patterns

Figure 13: Failure patterns

The failure pattern shows how valid the result is
  • Always photograph the specimen
  • Diagonal shear: a clean, well-defined failure plane
  • Barreling: bulging without a clear plane
  • Vertical cracks: brittle or fissured soil, so treat the number with care

From raw data to results

Figure 16: Reading the curve

QuantityFormula
Axial strain$\varepsilon=\Delta L/L_0$
Corrected area$A_c=A_0/(1-\varepsilon)$
Axial stress$\sigma=P/A_c$
Strength$q_u=\sigma_{max}$ (or $\sigma$ at 15% strain)
Undrained shear strength$c_u=q_u/2$ (saturated soil, undrained)
Stiffness$E_{50}$ = secant slope to $q_u/2$

The area is corrected because the specimen bulges as it shortens, so the loaded area grows (constant specimen volume assumed). Plot σ against ε for every reading, not only the peak. The numbers are worked through on the worked-example slide.

Stress–strain curve

Figure 17: Stress–strain curve

Highest point = $q_u$
  • Secant slope to 50% of $q_u$ → $E_{50}=0.5q_u/\varepsilon_{50}$
  • Peak at about 4% strain in our example

What do you see?

  • Stage 1 (nearly linear): the specimen shortens and stress rises at a nearly constant slope (related to $E_{50}$).
  • Peak: at $\varepsilon\approx4\%$ (for w = 21%) the stress reaches $q_u$ and the soil structure starts to fail. Wetter soil peaks at a larger strain and a lower $q_u$.
  • Strain softening: after the peak, as the failure plane develops, strength drops.

What can we read from the curve?

Figure 19: Reading the curve

$q_u$

Compressive strength: the highest point of the curve.

$c_u=q_u/2$

Undrained shear strength of saturated soil: half the compressive strength.

$E_{50}$

Soil stiffness: secant slope from the origin to 50% of $q_u$. Higher means stiffer soil.

Mohr circle

Figure 20: Mohr circle (worked-example values)

Diameter of the circle = $q_u$; radius = $c_u$
  1. Draw it. No confining stress ($\sigma_3=0$) and peak stress $\sigma_1=q_u$ give a circle of diameter $q_u=52.5$ kPa.
  2. Read it. The radius is the undrained shear strength: $c_u=q_u/2\approx 26.3$ kPa.
  3. Check it. The circle touches the horizontal envelope ($\varphi_u=0$) at $2\theta=90$°: failure plane at 45°.

Assumes saturated, undrained soil. In unsaturated field soil $c_u=q_u/2$ is only approximate.

Worked example (illustrative data)

Figure 21: Worked example

$q_u=52.5$ kPa → $c_u=26.3$ kPa
  • 1 · Area
    $$A_0=\dfrac{\pi D^2}{4}=\dfrac{\pi\,(38)^2}{4}=1134\ \text{mm}^2$$
  • 2 · Strain
    $$\varepsilon=\dfrac{\Delta L}{L_0}=\dfrac{3.04}{76}=0.04\ (4\%)$$
  • 3 · Corrected area
    $$A_c=\dfrac{A_0}{1-\varepsilon}=\dfrac{1134}{1-0.04}=1181\ \text{mm}^2$$
  • 4 · Strength
    $$q_u=\dfrac{P}{A_c}=\dfrac{62\ \text{N}}{1181\ \text{mm}^2}=0.0525\ \text{MPa}=52.5\ \text{kPa}$$
  • 5 · Shear strength
    $$c_u=\dfrac{q_u}{2}=\dfrac{52.5}{2}=26.3\ \text{kPa}$$
  • Result: "medium" consistency (50–100 kPa)

The corrected area allows for bulging as the specimen shortens. Units: 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa = 1000 kPa.

Consistency classes

Figure 22: Consistency classes

Classes based on $q_u$ (kPa)
  • Worked example: 52.5 kPa → "medium"
  • Class limits: Terzaghi & Peck (1967)
  • Higher class → stronger soil: more draft force, harder for roots
  • Report the class together with $q_u$ and the water content

Field decisions from one number

Figure 23: Tillage: the tool shears a wedge of soil ahead of it

Higher soil strength → higher draft force
  • The tool must shear a wedge of soil ahead of it, so the force grows with $c_u=q_u/2$ (Gill & Vanden Berg, 1967)
  • Measure $q_u$ at field moisture to choose tool shape, depth and speed
  • Soil strength changes with moisture, so the same field needs different draft on different days

Figure 24: Roots

Compacted soil → short, thick roots
  • Roots must push soil aside: the higher $q_u$, the harder it is
  • A hard layer stops deep growth, so roots turn sideways
  • Less water and nutrient uptake from the deeper soil

Figure 25: Seedling emergence through a crust

Crust strength determines crop establishment
  • Crust with high $q_u$ → weak emergence
  • A seedling that cannot break through bends sideways and often fails to establish
  • Breaking the crust → surface moisture management

Key takeaways

Moisture and density control soil strength; one test turns that into a number you can act on.

qu

Peak stress of the unconfined specimen (or stress at 15% strain if there is no peak).

cu = qu/2

Undrained shear strength, valid for saturated cohesive soil.

w, ρd, n ≥ 3

Always report water content, dry density and at least three replicates with qu.

Use the consistency class together with moisture to decide on traffic, tillage and seedbed work.

Soil texture

Figure 26: Soil texture

Clay is cohesive; sand is not
  • UCT suits cohesive soil
  • Clay particles bind together (cohesion), so a specimen stands by itself
  • Sand and gravel have no cohesion: the specimen collapses without confinement
